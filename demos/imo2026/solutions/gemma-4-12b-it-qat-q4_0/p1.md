To solve the problem, we analyze the effects of Confucius's moves on the set of integers on the blackboard.

**Part (a): Existence of a Unique $M > 1$**
Let the integers on the blackboard at any step be $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{2026}$, where each $x_i > 1$. In one move, Confucius chooses $x_j, x_k > 1$ and replaces them with $x_j' = \gcd(x_j, x_k)$ and $x_k' = \frac{\text{lcm}(x_j, x_k)}{\gcd(x_j, x_k)}$. 

First, let's consider the product of the integers $P = \prod_{i=1}^{2026} x_i$. In each move, the product changes from $P$ to $P' = \frac{P}{x_j x_k} \cdot x_j' x_k'$. Since $x_j' x_k' = \gcd(x_j, x_k) \cdot \frac{\text{lcm}(x_j, x_k)}{\gcd(x_j, x_k)} = \text{lcm}(x_j, x_k)$, the new product is $P' = \frac{P \cdot \text{lcm}(x_j, x_k)}{x_j x_k} = \frac{P}{\gcd(x_j, x_k)}$.
Since $\gcd(x_j, x_k) \geq 1$, we have $P' \leq P$. The product $P$ is non-increasing. Moreover, if $\gcd(x_j, x_k) > 1$, then $P' < P$. If $\gcd(x_j, x_k) = 1$, then $P' = P$ and one of the new numbers is $x_j' = \gcd(x_j, x_k) = 1$.

Notice that the number of integers greater than $1$ on the blackboard is non-increasing. In each move, either the number of integers greater than $1$ decreases (if $\gcd(x_j, x_k) = 1$ or if $x_k' = 1$) or the product $P$ strictly decreases. Since $P$ is a positive integer and $P > 1$ (as all initial $x_i > 1$), the process must terminate after finitely many moves. At the end, at most one integer can be greater than $1$. Because $P$ cannot become $1$ unless at least one number was 1 (which we can show by $P_t = P_{t-1}/\gcd(x_j, x_k) \geq \frac{x_j x_k}{\gcd(x_j, x_k)} = \text{lcm}(x_j, x_k) > 1$), there must be exactly one integer $M > 1$ on the blackboard.

**Part (b): Independence of $M$**
Consider the prime factorization of each integer $x_i = \prod_p p^{e_{i,p}}$, where $e_{i,p} \geq 0$ is the exponent of prime $p$. For a fixed prime $p$, let the exponents be $e_{1,p}, e_{2,p}, \dots, e_{2026,p}$. In a move involving $x_j$ and $x_k$, the exponents $e_{j,p}$ and $e_{k,p}$ are replaced by:
$$e_{j,p}' = \min(e_{j,p}, e_{k,p}) \quad \text{and} \quad e_{k,p}' = |e_{j,p} - e_{k,p}|$$
We observe that the greatest common divisor of the set of exponents for a fixed prime $p$ is invariant:
$$\gcd(e_{j,p}, e_{k,p}) = \gcd(\min(e_{j,p}, e_{k,p}), |e_{j,p} - e_{k,p}|)$$
This is a standard property of the Euclidean algorithm. Thus, for each prime $p$, the value $g_p = \gcd(e_{1,p}, e_{2,p}, \dots, e_{2026,p})$ is invariant throughout the process.

At the end of the process, there is exactly one integer $M > 1$, say $M = x_k$, while all other $x_i = 1$. For $x_i = 1$, all its exponents are zero ($e_{i,p} = 0$ for all $i \neq k$). Therefore, for each prime $p$:
$$g_p = \gcd(e_{1,p}, e_{2,p}, \dots, e_{k,p}, \dots, e_{2026,p}) = \gcd(0, 0, \dots, e_{k,p}, \dots, 0) = e_{k,p}$$
This implies that the exponent of $p$ in the prime factorization of $M$ is exactly $g_p$. Thus, $M = \prod_p p^{g_p}$. Since each $g_p$ is uniquely determined by the initial integers, $M$ does not depend on the choices made by Confucius.
