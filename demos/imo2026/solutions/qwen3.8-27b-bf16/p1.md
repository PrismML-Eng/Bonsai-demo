**Solution.**  
Let \(N=2026\). For a prime \(p\) and a positive integer \(x\), write \(\nu_p(x)\) for the exponent of \(p\) in \(x\). We use the standard conventions
\[
\gcd(0,0)=0,\qquad \gcd(a,0)=a.
\]

We shall prove both parts.

---

## (a) Termination and the existence of a unique integer \(M>1\)

Let

- \(P\) be the product of all integers currently written on the blackboard,
- \(C\) be the number of entries on the blackboard that are greater than \(1\).

Initially \(P>1\) and \(C=N=2026\).

Consider one move. Suppose Confucius chooses two entries \(m,n>1\), and let
\[
d=\gcd(m,n).
\]
The two numbers replace \(m,n\) by
\[
d
\quad\text{and}\quad
\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{d}
=\frac{mn}{d^2}.
\]

### Effect on the product

The product of the two new numbers is
\[
d\cdot \frac{mn}{d^2}=\frac{mn}{d}.
\]
Therefore the total product changes from \(P\) to
\[
P'=\frac{P}{d}.
\]
Thus:

- if \(d=1\), the product does not change;
- if \(d>1\), the product strictly decreases.

Since \(P\) is always a positive integer, it can strictly decrease only finitely many times.

### Effect on the number of entries greater than \(1\)

We examine how many of the two new numbers are greater than \(1\).

1. **If \(d=1\)**, then the new numbers are
   \[
   1 \quad\text{and}\quad mn.
   \]
   Since \(m,n>1\), we have \(mn>1\). Hence exactly one of the two new numbers is greater than \(1\), so \(C\) decreases by \(1\).

2. **If \(d>1\) and \(m\neq n\)**, then \(d>1\), and also
   \[
   \frac{mn}{d^2}=\frac{m}{d}\cdot \frac{n}{d}>1,
   \]
   because \(\frac{m}{d},\frac{n}{d}\) are positive integers and not both equal to \(1\). Hence both new numbers are greater than \(1\), so \(C\) stays the same.

3. **If \(m=n\)**, then \(d=m>1\), and
   \[
   \frac{mn}{d^2}=1.
   \]
   Hence the new numbers are
   \[
   m \quad\text{and}\quad 1,
   \]
   so exactly one is greater than \(1\), and \(C\) decreases by \(1\).

Thus in every move, \(C\) either stays the same or decreases by \(1\). In particular, \(C\) can decrease only finitely many times, and it never becomes \(0\): a move always replaces two entries \(>1\) by at least one entry \(>1\).

### Finiteness of the process

Now every move is of one of the following two types:

- \(d=1\): then \(C\) decreases;
- \(d>1\): then \(P\) strictly decreases.

Since \(C\) can decrease only finitely many times and \(P\), being a positive integer, can strictly decrease only finitely many times, no infinite sequence of moves is possible. Therefore the process stops after finitely many moves.

When the process stops, no move is possible, so there cannot be two entries greater than \(1\). Hence \(C\le 1\). But we have shown that \(C\) never becomes \(0\). Therefore
\[
C=1.
\]
So exactly one integer on the blackboard is greater than \(1\). Call that integer \(M\).

This proves part (a).

---

## (b) Independence of \(M\) from the choices

Fix a prime \(p\). At any moment, if the entries on the blackboard are
\[
x_1,x_2,\dots,x_N,
\]
consider the exponents
\[
e_i=\nu_p(x_i)\qquad (i=1,\dots,N).
\]
Define
\[
G=\gcd(e_1,e_2,\dots,e_N).
\]

We claim that \(G\) is invariant under the allowed moves.

Suppose a move is made on two entries \(x_i,x_j\). Let
\[
a=\nu_p(x_i),\qquad b=\nu_p(x_j).
\]
The new entries are \(\gcd(x_i,x_j)\) and \(\dfrac{\operatorname{lcm}(x_i,x_j)}{\gcd(x_i,x_j)}\). Their \(p\)-adic valuations are respectively
\[
\nu_p(\gcd(x_i,x_j))=\min(a,b)
\]
and
\[
\nu_p\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(x_i,x_j)}{\gcd(x_i,x_j)}\right)
=\max(a,b)-\min(a,b)=|a-b|.
\]
Thus the two exponents \(a,b\) are replaced by
\[
\min(a,b),\qquad |a-b|.
\]

For nonnegative integers \(a,b\), we have
\[
\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(\min(a,b),|a-b|).
\]
Indeed, if \(a\le b\), then
\[
\gcd(a,b)=\gcd(a,b-a)=\gcd(\min(a,b),|a-b|),
\]
and the other case is symmetric.

Therefore replacing \(a,b\) by \(\min(a,b),|a-b|\) does not change the gcd of the two numbers, and hence does not change the gcd of the whole list
\[
e_1,e_2,\dots,e_N.
\]
All other exponents are unchanged, so \(G\) is invariant throughout the entire process.

Now consider the final state. By part (a), the blackboard contains
\[
M,1,1,\dots,1.
\]
Therefore the list of \(p\)-adic valuations at the end is
\[
\nu_p(M),0,0,\dots,0,
\]
whose gcd is exactly \(\nu_p(M)\). By invariance, this must equal the initial value of \(G\). Hence
\[
\nu_p(M)=\gcd\bigl(\nu_p(x_{1,0}),\nu_p(x_{2,0}),\dots,\nu_p(x_{N,0})\bigr),
\]
where \(x_{1,0},x_{2,0},\dots,x_{N,0}\) are the original \(2026\) integers.

This holds for every prime \(p\). Consequently,
\[
M=\prod_{p} p^{\,G_p},
\]
where
\[
G_p=\gcd\bigl(\nu_p(x_{1,0}),\nu_p(x_{2,0}),\dots,\nu_p(x_{N,0})\bigr).
\]
Only finitely many primes have \(G_p>0\), because only finitely many primes divide the original \(2026\) integers. Thus this product is finite and well-defined.

The value of \(M\) is therefore completely determined by the initial \(2026\) integers, and does not depend on Confucius’s choices of moves.

This proves part (b). \(\square\)
