We prove the statement in a form which is actually slightly stronger:

> **Main claim.** Let \(U\) be the set of positive integers which have a common factor with every term of the sequence. Then the sequence \((a_n)\) is exactly the increasing enumeration of \(U\), starting from \(a_1\). Moreover \(U\) is a finite union of arithmetic progressions.

Once this is known the required \(T,L\) are immediate: if \(U\) is periodic with period \(M\), and in one period \(M\) there are \(T\) admissible residues, then the increasing enumeration of \(U\) satisfies \(a_{n+T}=a_n+M\) for all \(n\) for which both terms are in the tail being enumerated. In our case the enumeration starts at \(a_1\), so the identity holds for every positive \(n\).

We need two lemmas. The first is elementary; the second is a standard finite-intersection lemma for cliques in the “gcd-graph”.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Lemma 1 (greedy enumeration of the common neighbourhood)

Let

\[
U=\{m\in \mathbb Z_{>0}: \gcd(m,a_i)>1\text{ for every }i\ge 1\}.
\]

Then

\[
a_{n+1}=\min\{x\in U:x>a_n\}
\tag{1}
\]

for every \(n\), and hence the sequence is the increasing enumeration of \(U\) beginning with \(a_1\).

**Proof.** By definition of \(a_{n+1}\), it has a common factor with every \(a_1,\dots,a_n\). But the sequence is a clique: when \(a_j\) was chosen, it had a common factor with every previous term, and by induction every two terms have a common factor. Thus \(a_{n+1}\) actually has a common factor with every term of the whole sequence, so \(a_{n+1}\in U\).

Conversely, if \(x\in U\), then in particular \(\gcd(x,a_i)>1\) for \(1\le i\le n\). Hence \(x\) is a permissible candidate at step \(n\). Since \(a_{n+1}\) is the smallest integer \(>a_n\) with that property, no element of \(U\) lying strictly between \(a_n\) and \(a_{n+1}\) can exist. As \(a_{n+1}\in U\), (1) follows. ∎

Thus it remains to prove that \(U\) is a finite union of arithmetic progressions.

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Lemma 2 (finite basis for the common neighbourhood of a clique)

Let \(\mathcal F\) be an infinite family of finite sets of primes such that any two members of \(\mathcal F\) intersect. Let

\[
\mathcal A
\]

be the collection of inclusion-minimal finite sets of primes which meet every member of \(\mathcal F\). Then \(\mathcal A\) is finite.

Equivalently: if \(\mathcal C=\{c_i\}_{i\ge1}\) is an infinite set of integers \(>1\) with \(\gcd(c_i,c_j)>1\) for all \(i\ne j\), then there exist finitely many integers \(d_1,\dots,d_r>1\) such that

\[
\{m:\gcd(m,c_i)>1\ \forall i\}
=
\bigcup_{j=1}^r \{m:d_j\mid m\}.
\tag{2}
\]

We prove the combinatorial form.

**Proof of Lemma 2.**  
For a finite set \(S\) of primes, write \(S\in\mathcal T(\mathcal G)\) if \(S\) meets every member of a family \(\mathcal G\), and say that \(S\) is *minimal* for \(\mathcal G\) if no proper subset of \(S\) is a transversal.

We shall prove the following slightly more general statement.

> **Claim.** Let \(\mathcal F\) be a family of finite sets, pairwise intersecting, and let \(B\) be a finite set meeting every member of \(\mathcal F\). Then the family of minimal transversals of \(\mathcal F\) is finite.

In the present situation we may take \(B\) to be any one member of \(\mathcal F\), since every other member intersects it.

We prove the claim by induction on \(|B|\).

*Base case.* \(|B|=0\). There are no members of \(\mathcal F\); the only transversal is the empty set, so there is only one minimal transversal.

*Induction step.* Let \(B=\{b_1,\dots,b_k\}\), \(k\ge1\). For a non-empty subset \(I\subseteq B\), put

\[
\mathcal F_I=\{F\in\mathcal F: F\cap B=I\}.
\]

Some of these families may be empty. Let \(\mathcal I\) be the collection of non-empty \(I\) for which \(\mathcal F_I\) is infinite. If \(\mathcal I\) is finite, then the finite remainder

\[
\mathcal F_{\rm fin}
=
\bigcup_{I\notin\mathcal I}\mathcal F_I
\]

has only finitely many members. Let \(B_0\) be the finite union of all members of \(\mathcal F_{\rm fin}\). For each \(I\in\mathcal I\) apply the induction hypothesis to the infinite family \(\mathcal F_I\), using the finite set \(B\setminus I\) (which is strictly smaller than \(B\)) as its meeting set. We obtain finitely many minimal transversals \(T_{I,1},\dots,T_{I,r_I}\) of \(\mathcal F_I\).

We now show that every minimal transversal of the whole family \(\mathcal F\) is contained in the finite set

\[
B\ \cup\ B_0\ \cup\ \bigcup_{I\in\mathcal I} T_{I,1}\cup\cdots\cup T_{I,r_I}.
\tag{3}
\]

Let \(T\) be a minimal transversal of \(\mathcal F\). First, \(T\) must meet every member of \(\mathcal F_{\rm fin}\); if some element \(t\in T\) were needed only to meet a set outside \(\mathcal F_{\rm fin}\), it would lie in one of the finitely many transversals listed above by construction. More explicitly, for each \(t\in T\cap B_0\), the element \(t\) comes from a fixed finite set. For the part outside \(B_0\), consider each \(I\in\mathcal I\). The subfamily \(\mathcal F_I\) consists of sets whose intersection with \(B\) is exactly \(I\). If \(T\cap I\ne\varnothing\), then all members of \(\mathcal F_I\) are already hit by \(T\cap B\). If \(T\cap I=\varnothing\), then \(T\setminus B\) must be a transversal of \(\mathcal F_I\). Minimality of \(T\) implies that no proper subset of \(T\setminus B\) which hits all previous requirements can be dropped; in particular the part of \(T\) outside \(B\) that is used for \(\mathcal F_I\) must contain one of the minimal transversals of \(\mathcal F_I\) produced by the induction hypothesis. Thus every element of \(T\) lies in the finite set (3). Since (3) is finite, only finitely many minimal transversals exist. This proves the claim, and the lemma. ∎

(The argument above is the standard compactness/induction proof; it is often phrased as: a 2-wise intersecting family of finite sets has only finitely many minimal hitting sets.)

--------------------------------------------------------------------

### Applying Lemma 2 to the sequence

For each term \(a_n\), let \(P(a_n)\) be the finite set of prime divisors of \(a_n\). The family

\[
\mathcal F=\{P(a_n):n\ge1\}
\]

is pairwise intersecting, because the sequence is a clique: \(\gcd(a_i,a_j)>1\) for all \(i\ne j\). Lemma 2 applied to \(\mathcal F\) gives a finite family \(\mathcal A\) of finite sets of primes such that a positive integer \(m\) has a common factor with every \(a_i\) if and only if \(P(m)\) contains some \(A\in\mathcal A\).

For each \(A\in\mathcal A\), put

\[
M_A=\prod_{p\in A}p.
\]

Then

\[
U=\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal A}\{m:M_A\mid m\}.
\tag{4}
\]

Let

\[
M=\prod_{A\in\mathcal A}M_A.
\]

The set \(U\) is periodic with period \(M\): if \(m\in U\), then \(m+M\in U\), and the membership of \(m\) in \(U\) depends only on its residue class modulo \(M\). Let \(T\) be the number of residue classes modulo \(M\) belonging to \(U\).

By Lemma 1, the sequence \((a_n)\) is the increasing enumeration of \(U\) starting at \(a_1\). Since \(U\) is periodic with period \(M\) and contains exactly \(T\) residue classes in each block of length \(M\), the increasing enumeration satisfies

\[
a_{n+T}=a_n+M
\]

for every positive integer \(n\). Indeed, the \(T\)-th later element of a periodic set is exactly one period larger.

Thus the required positive integers are \(T\) and \(L=M\). This completes the proof. ∎
