**Answer.**  
Mulan can guarantee victory in finitely many steps exactly for

\[
\boxed{\theta=\frac{180^\circ}{n}\quad\text{for some integer }n\ge 2.}
\]

Equivalently, \(180^\circ/\theta\) must be an integer.

We measure angles in degrees.

---

## 1. Sufficiency: if \(\theta=180^\circ/n\), Mulan has a finite winning strategy

Assume

\[
\theta=\frac{180^\circ}{n},\qquad n\in\mathbb Z,\ n\ge 2.
\]

Call an angle

\[
m\theta\qquad(1\le m\le n-1)
\]

a **good angle**. In particular, \(\theta\) itself is a good angle, and a triangle containing a good angle \(m\theta\) with \(m\ge2\) is close to a terminal position.

### Lemma 1: Reducing a good angle

If a triangle contains a good angle \(m\theta\) with \(m\ge 2\), then Mulan can force the appearance of angle \(\theta\) in finitely many further moves.

**Proof.** Cut from the vertex whose angle is \(m\theta\), splitting that angle into

\[
\theta\quad\text{and}\quad (m-1)\theta.
\]

This is possible because \(0<\theta<(m-1)\theta<m\theta\). The two resulting triangles then have angles

\[
(\theta,\, b,\, \text{third}),\qquad ((m-1)\theta,\, c,\, \text{third}').
\]

If Shan-Yu discards the triangle containing \(\theta\), the game is already won. If he discards that triangle, the remaining triangle contains the smaller good angle \((m-1)\theta\). Repeating this process decreases \(m\), eventually reaching \(m=1\), i.e. angle \(\theta\). \(\square\)

Thus, once the current triangle contains some good angle \(m\theta\) with \(m\ge 2\), Mulan is in a winning finite-position.

---

### Lemma 2: From any right triangle, make two good angles

Let \(k=\lfloor n/2\rfloor\), and define

\[
A=k\theta,\qquad B=(n-k)\theta.
\]

Then

\[
45^\circ<A\le 90^\circ,\qquad B=180^\circ-A\ge 90^\circ,
\]

and both \(A\) and \(B\) are good angles.

Now take a right triangle with angles

\[
90^\circ,\quad x,\quad 90^\circ-x,
\]

where \(0<x<90\). Cut from the right-angle vertex to a point on the opposite side, splitting the right angle into angles \(u\) and \(90^\circ-u\). The two resulting triangles then have angles

\[
(u,\ x,\ 180^\circ-x-u)
\]

and

\[
(90^\circ-u,\ 90^\circ-x,\ x+u).
\]

We choose \(u\) as follows.

- If \(x<A\), take

  \[
  u=A-x.
  \]

  Since \(0<x<A\le90^\circ\), we have \(0<u<90^\circ\). The two new triangles are

  \[
  (A-x,\ x,\ B)
  \]

  and

  \[
  (90^\circ-A+x,\ 90^\circ-x,\ A).
  \]

  Hence one contains \(B\), and the other contains \(A\).

- If \(x\ge A\), take

  \[
  u=B-x.
  \]

  Because \(A>45^\circ\), the condition \(x\ge A\) implies \(x>90^\circ-A\). Also \(x<90^\circ<B\). Thus \(0<u<90^\circ\). The two new triangles are

  \[
  (B-x,\ x,\ A)
  \]

  and

  \[
  (x-(90^\circ-A),\ 90^\circ-x,\ B).
  \]

  Hence again one contains \(B\), and the other contains \(A\).

Thus from every right triangle, Mulan can make a cut so that both resulting triangles contain a good angle. \(\square\)

---

### Lemma 3: From any non-right triangle, make two right triangles

Let \(T\) be a non-right triangle. It has a vertex whose two adjacent angles are both acute:

- if \(T\) is obtuse, take the obtuse vertex;
- if \(T\) is acute, take any vertex.

Let the angle at that chosen vertex be \(a\), and let the two adjacent angles be \(b,c<90^\circ\). Cut from that vertex, splitting \(a\) into

\[
u=90^\circ-b
\]

and

\[
a-u.
\]

This is legal because

\[
0<u<a
\]

since \(b<90^\circ\) and \(a+b=180^\circ-c>90^\circ\). The two resulting triangles have angles

\[
(90^\circ-b,\ b,\ 90^\circ)
\]

and

\[
(90^\circ-c,\ c,\ 90^\circ).
\]

So Mulan can always turn a non-right triangle into two right triangles. \(\square\)

---

### The overall strategy

Use the following rule at the beginning of each round.

1. If the current triangle already has an angle \(\theta\), the game has stopped.

2. Otherwise, if it contains a good angle \(m\theta\) with \(m\ge2\), apply Lemma 1.

3. Otherwise, if it is a right triangle, apply Lemma 2.

4. Otherwise, it is non-right; apply Lemma 3.

This is a well-defined strategy.

Why does it terminate in finitely many steps?

- If step 4 is used, the next triangle is right.
- If step 3 is used, the next triangle contains \(A\) or \(B\), both of which are good angles. If one of them is \(\theta\), the game stops immediately; otherwise the next triangle contains a good angle \(m\theta\) with \(m\ge2\), so step 2 becomes available.
- Step 2 then continues until \(\theta\) appears.

Thus after at most two moves of steps 3/4, Mulan either has already won or reaches a triangle containing a good angle. From that point on, Lemma 1 guarantees termination in finitely many moves. Therefore Mulan guarantees victory in finitely many steps, no matter how Shan-Yu discards. \(\square\)

---

## 2. Necessity: if \(180^\circ/\theta\) is not an integer, Mulan cannot guarantee victory

Assume now that

\[
\frac{180^\circ}{\theta}
\]

is **not** an integer. Define the finite set

\[
S=\{m\theta: m\in\mathbb Z,\ 0<m\theta<180^\circ\}.
\]

Every angle in \(S\) is a positive integer multiple of \(\theta\) less than \(180^\circ\). In particular, \(\theta\in S\).

Call a triangle **\(S\)-free** if none of its angles belongs to \(S\).

### Existence of an \(S\)-free initial triangle

The set of forbidden values

\[
x\in(0,90^\circ)\quad\text{such that }x\in S
\text{ or }180^\circ-2x\in S
\]

is finite. Hence we can choose \(x\in(0,90^\circ)\) avoiding it. The isosceles triangle with angles

\[
x,\quad x,\quad 180^\circ-2x
\]

then has no angle in \(S\). Thus an \(S\)-free initial triangle exists.

---

### Invariance lemma

If a triangle is \(S\)-free, then after any legal cut by Mulan, at least one of the two resulting triangles is still \(S\)-free.

**Proof.** Let the original triangle have angles

\[
a,\ b,\ c
\]

with none of them in \(S\). Cut from the vertex with angle \(a\), splitting \(a\) into \(u\) and \(a-u\), where \(0<u<a\). The two resulting triangles have angle triples

\[
(u,\ b,\ a+c-u)
\]

and

\[
(a-u,\ c,\ b+u).
\]

Suppose, for contradiction, that both resulting triangles contain an angle in \(S\). Since \(b,c\notin S\), the \(S\)-angle in the first triangle must be either \(u\) or \(a+c-u\), and the \(S\)-angle in the second must be either \(a-u\) or \(b+u\).

There are four cases.

1. \(u\in S\) and \(a-u\in S\).  
   Then
   \[
   a=u+(a-u)
   \]
   is a positive integer multiple of \(\theta\) less than \(180^\circ\), so \(a\in S\), contradiction.

2. \(u\in S\) and \(b+u\in S\).  
   Then
   \[
   b=(b+u)-u
   \]
   is a positive integer multiple of \(\theta\) less than \(180^\circ\), so \(b\in S\), contradiction.

3. \(a+c-u\in S\) and \(a-u\in S\).  
   Then
   \[
   c=(a+c-u)-(a-u)
   \]
   is a positive integer multiple of \(\theta\) less than \(180^\circ\), so \(c\in S\), contradiction.

4. \(a+c-u\in S\) and \(b+u\in S\).  
   Then
   \[
   (a+c-u)+(b+u)=a+b+c=180^\circ.
   \]
   Hence \(180^\circ\) is an integer multiple of \(\theta\), contradicting the assumption that \(180^\circ/\theta\) is not an integer.

All possibilities are impossible. Therefore at least one of the two resulting triangles is \(S\)-free. \(\square\)

---

### Shan-Yu’s strategy

Shan-Yu chooses initially an \(S\)-free triangle. Thereafter, after every cut, he keeps an \(S\)-free triangle. By the invariance lemma, such a triangle always exists.

Since every \(S\)-free triangle has no angle in \(S\), and \(\theta\in S\), the remaining triangle never has an angle equal to \(\theta\). Thus the game never stops.

So Mulan cannot guarantee victory in finitely many steps when \(180^\circ/\theta\) is not an integer. \(\square\)

---

Combining the two parts, Mulan can guarantee victory in finitely many steps exactly when

\[
\boxed{\theta=\frac{180^\circ}{n}\text{ for some integer }n\ge2.}
\]
