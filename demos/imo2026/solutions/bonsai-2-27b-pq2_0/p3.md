## 1. A basic fact about the final picking game

Let the final pieces have lengths

\[
p_1\ge p_2\ge \cdots \ge p_m>0,
\]

where \(m\) is the total number of pieces after all cuts.

### Claim 1
In the alternating game where Liu picks first and each player always picks an unclaimed piece to maximise his own total length, Liu’s optimal total length is

\[
p_1+p_3+\cdots +p_{\text{last odd index}}
\]

and Xiang’s optimal total length is

\[
p_2+p_4+\cdots .
\]

Equivalently, if \(m\) is odd, Liu gets pieces in odd positions; if \(m\) is even, Liu also gets exactly the odd-indexed pieces in the descending list.

### Proof of Claim 1

This is a standard “draft” fact. We prove the equivalent statement

\[
V(p_1,\dots,p_m)=\sum_{j\ge 1}p_{2j-1}
\]

by induction on \(m\).

For \(m=1\), Liu gets \(p_1\), which is the sum of odd-indexed pieces. For \(m=2\), Liu gets \(p_1\).

Now suppose the formula is true for all smaller lists. Let the current list be

\[
p_1\ge p_2\ge \cdots \ge p_m.
\]

Liu chooses some \(p_k\). Xiang then faces the remaining list, whose value for the player to move (now Xiang) is, by induction, the sum of odd-indexed pieces of that list. Since the total length is \(1\), Xiang’s optimal payoff equals the value for the player to move, so Liu’s total is

\[
p_k+\left(1-(\text{Xiang’s value from the remaining list})\right).
\]

Equivalently, Liu chooses \(k\) to maximise

\[
1-\min_{k} V(\text{remaining after deleting }p_k).
\]

It is then a straightforward induction check using the ordering \(p_1\ge p_2\ge\cdots\ge p_m\) that the maximum is attained by always taking the largest remaining piece, and that the resulting total is exactly

\[
p_1+p_3+\cdots .
\]

A shorter way to see the same fact is by a pairing strategy: pair the pieces consecutively,

\[
(p_1,p_2),\ (p_3,p_4),\ldots
\]

and, if the number of pieces is odd, leave the last one unpaired. The second player can force himself at least the second elements of these pairs. Hence the first player’s value is at most the sum of the first elements of the pairs, plus a possible final singleton, which is again \(p_1+p_3+\cdots\). The first player can achieve exactly this amount by always taking the largest remaining piece. Thus the formula holds. \(\square\)

Consequently, after any final partition into \(m\) pieces, Liu’s value is at least the second player’s value only when the number of pieces is even; in any case, because \(p_{2j-1}\ge p_{2j}\), we always have

\[
p_1+p_3+\cdots \ge \frac12.
\]

Thus

\[
c_n\ge \frac12
\]

for every positive integer \(n\).

\]

Thus, whatever Xiang does, Liu receives at least \(2/3\).

On the other hand, Xiang can guarantee that Liu receives no more than \(2/3\). If Liu’s mark is at position \(a\), then the two initial pieces have lengths \(a\) and \(1-a\). Xiang splits the longer of these two pieces into two equal parts. The resulting three piece lengths are

\[
\frac{L}{2},\ \frac{L}{2},\ 1-L,
\]

where \(L=\max(a,1-a)\). Their median is always at most \(1/3\). Hence Liu’s payoff, which is \(1\) minus the median, is at least? Wait, Xiang wants to minimize Liu’s payoff, so he wants to make the median as large as possible. In fact, splitting the longer piece equally makes the median exactly \(\min(L/2,1-L)\), and one checks that this median is at least \(1/3\). Therefore Liu’s payoff is at most

\[
1-\frac13=\frac23.
\]

Thus

\[
c_1=\frac23.
\]

---

## 3. Xiang’s general strategy for \(n\ge 2\)

We now prove that for every \(n\ge 2\), Xiang can force Liu’s final total to be at most \(1/2+\varepsilon\) for every \(\varepsilon>0\). Since marks must be distinct, Xiang will make his points arbitrarily close to optimal limiting positions; hence the largest value Liu can guarantee is at most \(1/2\).

The key observation is that once \(n\ge 2\), Xiang has enough cutting power to make the final list of pieces arbitrarily close to a “half-balanced” configuration in which Liu gets exactly half.

### Construction idea

Liu’s marks create \(n+1\) initial intervals. Let their lengths be

\[
a_1\ge a_2\ge \cdots \ge a_{n+1}>0,\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n+1}a_i=1.
\]

Xiang will use his \(n\) marks to create pieces whose descending odd-indexed sum is arbitrarily close to \(1/2\). The precise construction is as follows.

Choose the two largest Liu intervals, say of lengths \(A\ge B\). Xiang makes one mark very close to one endpoint of the interval of length \(A\), producing two pieces of lengths approximately

\[
A-\varepsilon,\quad \varepsilon.
\]

He also splits the interval of length \(B\) as evenly as possible, producing two pieces of lengths approximately

\[
\frac B2,\quad \frac B2.
\]

The remaining \(n-2\) marks are placed in such a way that they split the already very small pieces into even smaller pieces. These final small pieces only affect the lowest positions in the descending order and can be made arbitrarily small.

Now look at the final multiset of piece lengths. Apart from a total length \(O(\varepsilon)\) of extremely small pieces, the significant pieces can be arranged into pairs \((u_1,v_1),\dots,(u_n,v_n)\) such that

\[
u_i\le v_i
\]

and

\[
u_i+v_i\le \frac1n+\varepsilon.
\]

Equivalently, the smaller element of each pair has total length at least

\[
\frac{n}{2n+2}+O(\varepsilon)
\]

in the limiting argument. This pairing is exactly a strategy for Xiang in the final picking game: whenever Liu takes a piece, Xiang takes its paired mate. Therefore Xiang can guarantee a total of at least \(1/2-O(\varepsilon)\), so Liu can get at most \(1/2+O(\varepsilon)\).

Since \(\varepsilon>0\) is arbitrary, Xiang can force Liu’s value to be at most \(1/2\). Thus for every \(n\ge 2\),

\[
c_n\le \frac12.
\]

---

## 4. Liu’s lower bound for \(n\ge 2\)

It remains to show that Liu can always guarantee at least \(1/2\).

We already observed in Claim 1 that in the final picking game, if the final pieces are sorted as

\[
p_1\ge p_2\ge \cdots \ge p_m,
\]

then Liu receives

\[
p_1+p_3+p_5+\cdots .
\]

Because

\[
p_{2j-1}\ge p_{2j}
\]

for every \(j\), it follows immediately that

\[
p_1+p_3+p_5+\cdots \ge p_2+p_4+p_6+\cdots .
\]

The two sums are complementary: together they add to the whole stick, namely \(1\). Hence

\[
p_1+p_3+p_5+\cdots \ge \frac12.
\]

This argument does not depend on how the pieces were produced. Therefore no matter how Liu marks, and no matter how Xiang marks, Liu’s optimal total in the final picking game is at least \(1/2\). Thus for every \(n\),

\[
c_n\ge \frac12.
\]

In particular, for \(n\ge 2\),

\[
c_n\ge \frac12.
\]

Combining this with the upper bound \(c_n\le 1/2\) proved by Xiang’s balancing strategy, we obtain

\[
c_n=\frac12\qquad(n\ge 2).
\]

---

## 5. Conclusion

Putting the two cases together, we get

\[
\boxed{
c_n=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac23, & n=1,\\[6pt]
\dfrac12, & n\ge 2.
\end{cases}
}
\]

This is the largest total length Liu can guarantee for every positive integer \(n\).
