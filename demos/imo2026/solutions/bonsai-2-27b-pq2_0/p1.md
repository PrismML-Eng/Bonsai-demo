Let \(N=2026\). Write the numbers currently on the blackboard as
\[
a_1,a_2,\dots,a_N.
\]

We use the standard convention that the gcd of a list of nonnegative integers is \(0\) if all of them are \(0\).

---

## (a) Termination with exactly one integer \(>1\)

Let

\[
C=\#\{i: a_i>1\}
\]

be the number of entries greater than \(1\), and let

\[
P=a_1a_2\cdots a_N
\]

be their product.

Consider one move, and let the two chosen integers be \(x\) and \(y\), with

\[
d=\gcd(x,y).
\]

Then

\[
\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(x,y)}{\gcd(x,y)}
=\frac{xy/d}{d}
=\frac{xy}{d^2}.
\]

So the two new integers are

\[
d \quad\text{and}\quad \frac{xy}{d^2}.
\]

The local product changes from

\[
xy
\]

to

\[
d\cdot \frac{xy}{d^2}=\frac{xy}{d}.
\]

Hence the whole product changes as

\[
P\longmapsto \frac{P}{d}.
\]

Therefore:

- if \(d=1\), the product \(P\) is unchanged;
- if \(d>1\), the product \(P\) strictly decreases.

Now examine how \(C\) changes.

### Case 1: \(d=1\)

The two new integers are

\[
1 \quad\text{and}\quad xy.
\]

Since \(x,y>1\), we have \(xy>1\). Thus exactly one of the two replaced integers is \(>1\), so \(C\) decreases by \(1\).

### Case 2: \(x=y\)

Then \(d=x\), and

\[
\frac{xy}{d^2}=\frac{x^2}{x^2}=1.
\]

So the two new integers are

\[
x \quad\text{and}\quad 1.
\]

Again exactly one of them is \(>1\), so \(C\) decreases by \(1\).

### Case 3: \(d>1\) and \(x\ne y\)

Then \(d>1\). Also \(\frac{xy}{d^2}>1\), because if \(\frac{xy}{d^2}=1\), writing \(x=dr\), \(y=ds\) with positive integers \(r,s\), would give \(rs=1\), hence \(r=s=1\), so \(x=y\), a contradiction.

Thus both new integers are greater than \(1\), so \(C\) is unchanged. In this case \(d>1\), so \(P\) strictly decreases.

We have therefore shown:

\[
C \text{ never increases, and whenever } C \text{ stays unchanged, } P \text{ strictly decreases.}
\]

Now \(C\) is a positive integer bounded above by \(2026\). It can decrease only finitely many times. After it has stopped decreasing, every further move keeps \(C\) constant, and therefore every further move strictly decreases the positive integer \(P\). This is impossible indefinitely.

Hence the process must stop after finitely many moves.

When it stops, there is no move possible, so there is not at least one pair of entries both greater than \(1\). Thus at most one integer on the blackboard is greater than \(1\).

But at least one integer greater than \(1\) always remains: a move replaces two integers greater than \(1\) by two integers of which at least one is greater than \(1\), and all other entries remain unchanged. Since initially all \(2026\) integers are greater than \(1\), the number of entries greater than \(1\) can never become \(0\).

Therefore exactly one integer greater than \(1\) remains. Call it \(M\).

---

## (b) The value of \(M\) is independent of the choices

We now prove uniqueness of the final value.

For a prime \(p\), write \(\nu_p(n)\) for the exponent of \(p\) in the prime factorization of \(n\). Thus \(\nu_p(1)=0\).

For a given blackboard state, define

\[
G_p=\gcd\bigl(\nu_p(a_1),\nu_p(a_2),\dots,\nu_p(a_N)\bigr).
\]

We claim that for each prime \(p\), the number \(G_p\) is invariant under every move.

Suppose a move is made on two integers \(x\) and \(y\). Let

\[
u=\nu_p(x), \qquad v=\nu_p(y).
\]

For the new integer \(\gcd(x,y)\), the exponent of \(p\) is

\[
\nu_p(\gcd(x,y))=\min(u,v).
\]

For the new integer \(\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(x,y)}{\gcd(x,y)}\), the exponent of \(p\) is

\[
\max(u,v)-\min(u,v)=|u-v|.
\]

Thus the two new \(p\)-exponents are, up to order,

\[
\min(u,v) \quad\text{and}\quad |u-v|.
\]

But

\[
\gcd(u,v)=\gcd(\min(u,v),|u-v|).
\]

Indeed, if \(u\ge v\), then \(\gcd(u,v)=\gcd(v,u-v)\), and the case \(v\ge u\) is symmetric; the zero cases are immediate as well.

Hence the gcd of the two \(p\)-exponents involved in the move is unchanged. Since the other \(N-2\) \(p\)-exponents are also unchanged, the gcd of all \(N\) \(p\)-exponents is unchanged.

Therefore \(G_p\) is invariant throughout the process.

Now consider the final state. By part (a), the blackboard contains exactly one integer greater than \(1\), namely \(M\), and the remaining \(2025\) integers are \(1\). Thus for every prime \(p\), the list of \(p\)-exponents is

\[
\nu_p(M),0,0,\dots,0.
\]

Therefore

\[
G_p=\gcd(\nu_p(M),0,\dots,0)=\nu_p(M).
\]

But \(G_p\) is invariant, so \(\nu_p(M)\) must equal the value of \(G_p\) in the initial state.

Consequently, for every prime \(p\),

\[
\nu_p(M)=\gcd\bigl(\nu_p(a_1^{(0)}),\nu_p(a_2^{(0)}),\dots,\nu_p(a_{2026}^{(0)})\bigr),
\]

where \(a_1^{(0)},\dots,a_{2026}^{(0)}\) are the original integers.

Hence the final integer is uniquely determined:

\[
\boxed{
M=\prod_{p}
p^{\gcd\bigl(\nu_p(a_1^{(0)}),\dots,\nu_p(a_{2026}^{(0)})\bigr)}
}
\]

where the product is over all primes \(p\). Only finitely many primes occur with nonzero exponent, so this product is finite.

Since the exponent of each prime in \(M\) is completely determined by the initial board, the value of \(M\) does not depend on the choices made by Confucius.
