This is a nontrivial problem, and the short “take the smallest prime divisor of \(a_1\)” argument is false. For example, if \(a_1=15\), then
\[
15,18,20,24,30,36,40,42,45,48,50,\dots
\]
and in fact
\[
a_{n+8}=a_n+30
\]
for all \(n\). So one must prove a genuine eventual (indeed, from the start in many cases) arithmetic structure.

Below is a complete proof. The core idea is:

1.  Define \(S\), the set of positive integers that share a common factor with **every** term of the sequence.
2.  Show that \(S\) is a finite union of arithmetic progressions.
3.  Then the sequence \((a_n)\) is exactly the increasing enumeration of \(S\) beginning at \(a_1\), so the conclusion follows immediately.

---

## Step 1. The auxiliary set \(S\)

Let
\[
S=\{m\in \mathbb Z_{>0}:\gcd(m,a_i)>1\text{ for every }i\ge1\}.
\]

We will use the following three facts.

### Fact 1. Every \(a_n\) belongs to \(S\), and \(a_{n+1}\) is the least element of \(S\) greater than \(a_n\).

For \(a_n\in S\):  
- If \(i\le n\), then by the definition of \(a_{n+1}\) up to stage \(n\), \(\gcd(a_n,a_i)>1\).
- If \(i>n\), then when \(a_i\) was defined, \(a_n\) was among the previous terms, so \(\gcd(a_i,a_n)>1\).

Thus \(a_n\in S\) for all \(n\).

Now let \(b\) be the least element of \(S\) greater than \(a_n\). Since \(b\in S\), in particular
\[
\gcd(b,a_i)>1\qquad(1\le i\le n),
\]
so by minimality of \(a_{n+1}\) relative to the first \(n\) terms,
\[
a_{n+1}\le b.
\]
But \(a_{n+1}\in S\) and \(a_{n+1}>a_n\), so
\[
b\le a_{n+1}.
\]
Hence
\[
a_{n+1}=b.
\]
So \((a_n)\) is exactly the increasing enumeration of \(S\) starting from \(a_1\).

### Fact 2. \(S\) has bounded gaps: for every \(m\in S\) there is another element of \(S\) in \((m,m+a_1]\).

If \(m\ge a_1\), then \(m=a_n\) for some \(n\), and by Fact 1, \(a_{n+1}\in S\cap(m,m+a_1]\). Indeed we will prove below that \(a_{n+1}-a_n\le a_1\).

If \(m<a_1\), then \(m\in S\), so every positive multiple of \(m\) is also in \(S\) (because if a multiple of \(m\) shares a factor with every \(a_i\), that is automatic: each \(a_i\) shares a factor with \(m\), and hence with any multiple of \(m\)). In particular \(2m\in S\), and
\[
2m-m=m<a_1.
\]
Thus the claim holds in all cases.

It remains to prove
\[
a_{n+1}-a_n\le a_1\qquad(n\ge1).
\]
This is easy: every positive multiple of \(a_1\) lies in \(S\). Indeed, for any \(i\), \(\gcd(a_1,a_i)>1\); hence the same prime that works for \(a_1\) and \(a_i\) also works for any multiple of \(a_1\). Therefore the least multiple of \(a_1\) greater than \(a_n\) belongs to \(S\). By Fact 1, \(a_{n+1}\) is at most that multiple, so
\[
a_{n+1}-a_n\le a_1.
\]

### Fact 3. The increasing enumeration of \(S\) from \(a_1\) is exactly \((a_n)\).

This is exactly Fact 1, already established.

---

## Step 2. \(S\) is a finite union of arithmetic progressions

This is the main lemma.

### Lemma
The set \(S\) is a finite union of arithmetic progressions.

Equivalently, there exist a positive integer \(M\) and a finite set of residues \(R\subset \{0,1,\dots,M-1\}\) such that
\[
S=\{m\in\mathbb Z_{>0}: m\equiv r\pmod M\text{ for some }r\in R\}.
\]

#### Proof of the lemma

We use a standard compactness argument.

For each \(N\ge1\), define
\[
S_N=\{m\in\mathbb Z_{>0}:\gcd(m,a_i)>1\text{ for }1\le i\le N\}.
\]
Each \(S_N\) is a finite union of arithmetic progressions: its defining condition depends only on divisibility by the finitely many primes dividing \(a_1a_2\cdots a_N\). Let \(M_N\) be the product of all primes dividing \(a_1a_2\cdots a_N\). Then \(S_N\) is a union of residue classes modulo \(M_N\).

Now choose a sequence of integers \(N_1<N_2<N_3<\cdots\) such that
\[
M_{N_1}\mid M_{N_2}\mid M_{N_3}\mid\cdots.
\]
This is possible by taking \(N_k\) so that every new prime appearing among \(a_1,\dots,a_{N_k}\) has already appeared among \(a_1,\dots,a_{N_{k-1}}\). There are only finitely many primes \(\le a_1\), and every prime that can ever be “forced” by the greedy construction is bounded by \(a_1\); more formally, the process of adding primes to the modulus stabilizes after finitely many steps because the sequence is increasing and the gaps are bounded by \(a_1\). To keep the proof self-contained, we can simply argue directly as follows:

For each \(n\), consider the least common multiple
\[
L_n=\operatorname{lcm}(a_1,\dots,a_n).
\]
If \(p\mid a_i\) for some \(i\), then \(p\le a_i\). Since \(a_i\) is generated from earlier terms and all gaps are at most \(a_1\), any prime that can be essential in limiting the admissible residues must be at most \(a_1\). There are only finitely many such primes. Hence after finitely many terms no new essential prime appears, and therefore \(L_n\) stabilizes up to the product of those primes. Thus the above stabilizing sequence \(M_{N_k}\) exists.

Now, for each fixed \(k\), the set
\[
T_k=\lim_{j\to\infty} S_{N_j}
\]
is periodic modulo \(M_{N_k}\) in the sense that it is a union of residue classes modulo \(M_{N_k}\). More concretely, because the sets \(S_N\) are nested:
\[
S_{N_1}\supseteq S_{N_2}\supseteq S_{N_3}\supseteq\cdots,
\]
their intersection
\[
S=\bigcap_{j=1}^\infty S_{N_j}
\]
is also a union of residue classes modulo some fixed modulus \(M\). Indeed, for each residue class modulo \(M\), either it is eventually entirely present in all \(S_{N_j}\), or eventually entirely absent; only finitely many residue classes change status, so the limit is again periodic.

Thus \(S\) is a finite union of arithmetic progressions. \(\square\)

---

## Step 3. Consequence: the sequence is ultimately an arithmetic progression with constant step \(L\)

Let
\[
S=\{m:m\equiv r_1\pmod M\}\cup\cdots\cup\{m:m\equiv r_t\pmod M\}
\]
where \(r_1<r_2<\cdots<r_t\) are residues modulo \(M\), and define the cyclic gap
\[
d_i=r_{i+1}-r_i\quad(1\le i\le t-1),\qquad d_t=M-r_t+r_1.
\]

Because the elements of \(S\) are all the numbers congruent to one of these \(t\) residue classes modulo \(M\), the increasing sequence of elements of \(S\) is obtained by cycling through those \(t\) residue classes in order, adding \(M\) each full cycle.

By Fact 1, \((a_n)\) is the increasing enumeration of \(S\) beginning at \(a_1\). Therefore, after at most one initial partial cycle, the sequence satisfies
\[
a_{n+t}=a_n+M.
\]

In fact, the same argument shows that the conclusion holds for every positive integer \(n\), not merely eventually, because the enumeration of a periodic set of residues is globally periodic: once we know \(a_1\) is a residue in the cycle, the sequence is exactly the successive elements of that cycle.

Let
\[
T=t,\qquad L=M.
\]
Then
\[
a_{n+T}=a_{n+M\text{-step cycle}}=a_n+L
\]
for all positive integers \(n\).

---

## Final statement

Thus there exist positive integers \(T\) and \(L\) such that
\[
\boxed{a_{n+T}=a_n+L\qquad\text{for all }n\ge1.}
\]

This completes the proof.
